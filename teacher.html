Running the activity

Students sit on Jalyn's throne and rule on eleven citizens of Endel. The docket is shuffled on purpose. Seven end in Affabel and four in Lone, which breaks the pattern most students form after the first three cases.

Before they start

Tell them three things and nothing more. They are the judge, not the defendant. They will have to name the Scripture their ruling rests on, so a hunch will not get them through. And naming the right verse is only half of it: they then have to say what it means, and the wrong readings on offer are all ones real people actually hold. Do not tell them the ratio of outcomes. The surprise is the lesson.

Budget 50 to 70 minutes for all eleven cases. Each one takes five decisions: the verdict, the station, which texts apply, and then a reading for every text selected. Progress saves in the browser, so a student who runs out of time picks up on the same device. The verdict record prints or saves as a PDF for submission. If you only have one period, cases 1 through 6 are a complete arc on their own and end on the Social and Charity contrast.

Answer key

The five contrasts

The docket order separates each pair so the second one lands after students have already committed to a rule. These are where the discussion lives.

Charity and Social  ·  cases 6 and 11

Two restaurant owners. Both generous, both publicly admired, both in Affabel. One is governor of ten districts; the other is a plumber in the flatlands. Externally almost identical. Ask the class to name what actually separated them, and do not accept "one gave more" as the answer, because Social gave in public and often.

Motivator and Double Life  ·  cases 7 and 8

Two teachers. Both damaged students, and Motivator's former students are in Lone because of what he taught them. One is saved; one is in the deepest hole in the parable. The difference is not the size of the damage. Push until someone names it.

Patient and Selfish  ·  cases 4 and 10

The invisible secretary and the popular mayor. Ask what each of their performance reviews would have said in Endel, then what changed between that review and this one. Nothing about either life changed. Only the light it was read in.

Ruthless and Faint Heart  ·  cases 2 and 5

Seven days of obedience against a lifetime of profession. Handle Faint Heart carefully: she is a genuine victim and the parable says so. The question is not whether her wound was real but what she did with it for the rest of her life.

Brutal and Deceived  ·  cases 3 and 9

The abrasive convert everyone found tiresome, and the charming believer everyone liked. Ask whether anyone docked Brutal for being annoying, and where in the Book that criterion appears.

The trap text on every case

Each case offers five texts. The ones Jalyn used are listed above. Each pool also carries exactly one text he did not use, chosen because it is the verse students actually reach for when they get the case wrong. These are generated from the same file the activity runs on, so they cannot drift out of sync.

Discussion questions

  1. Jalyn tells Independent, "I am a merciful king, and that is exactly why I'm sending you away." What does he mean, and is it a fair use of the word mercy?
  2. Deceived and Ruthless both had the Ezekiel passage read over them, and it acquitted one and convicted the other. What is the passage actually measuring?
  3. Faint Heart says her heart hardened and "that wasn't my fault." Where in her story is the last point at which she could have chosen differently?
  4. Social's deeds fed real people. Jalyn says he already received his reward. Does that make the meals worth less to the people who ate them?
  5. Motivator never taught anything false. He only left things out. Is that a sin of commission or omission, and does the distinction survive James 3:1?
  6. Selfish sentenced himself to Lone before Jalyn spoke, and he was wrong. Was he wrong about what he deserved, or only about what he would receive?
  7. Patient's five thousand includes everyone Brutal reached. If your good is credited to whoever formed you, whose account are you currently adding to?
  8. The record tells you whether you ruled harsher or gentler than Jalyn. Which way did you lean, and what does that reveal about the God you are picturing?

A note on the harsh and gentle tally

This is usually the most productive thing on the record page. Students who ruled consistently harsher tend to be picturing a God who is looking for a reason to convict. Students who ruled consistently gentler tend to be picturing one who does not really mean the warnings. The parable refuses both. Consider collecting the tallies and putting the class distribution on the board before you discuss anything else.

Catholic framing

The parable is Protestant in origin and its Hall of Justice and Hall of Life map loosely onto particular judgment. Worth naming for students: the Catholic tradition holds the same two things this activity is built on, that our works are judged (CCC 1021-1022, 2 Cor 5:10) and that grace, not merit, is what saves (CCC 1996-2001, Eph 2:8-9). What the parable adds, and what Trent and the Catechism both affirm, is that the reward of the saved genuinely differs. Purgatory is the piece the allegory has no room for. That absence is worth pointing out rather than papering over.
